\section{Norse}

The Norse cosmos comes mainly from two sources. The prophecy of the seeress (the \textbf{Voluspa}) in the Poetic Edda. And the tricking of Gylfi (\textbf{Gylfaginning}) in Snorri's Prose Edda. They agree on the broad shape. The world rises from a void, raw matter forms, ordering powers shape it, and the whole order is destroyed and then reborn.

\subsection{The Creation Model}

In the beginning there is no earth and no sky. There is only a vast yawning emptiness. On one side lies a realm of frozen, unformed matter. On the other lies a realm of fire and heat. Cold flows in from one edge and heat flows in from the other.

Where the cold and the heat meet in the middle of the emptiness, the first being takes shape from the meltwater. It is a single immense body holding the raw matter of the world. From its own sweat more forms come. A nourishing source also forms from the thaw, and by feeding and uncovering it brings to light the ancestor of the ordering powers.

From that ancestor descend three shaping powers. The three slay the first being. They carry the body into the middle of the emptiness and build the world out of it. The flesh becomes the ground. The blood becomes the seas. The bones become the mountains. The skull becomes the sky. The brains become the clouds.

The shapers then find two lifeless forms washed up at the edge of the world. They give them breath, sense, and warmth, and these become the first humans. The whole structure hangs on a great tree whose trunk is the axis and whose roots reach the deep wells. The order is bound by a law of fate, and that law foretells a great ending in which the powers fall in battle and fire consumes the world. After the ending the ground rises green and new from the sea, and life returns.

\subsection{The Void and the Two Poles}

The void is the great yawning gap (\textbf{Ginnungagap}). It is bounded by two opposite realms.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.18\linewidth}
L{0.22\linewidth} L{0.4\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Pole} & \textbf{Realm} & \textbf{Nature} \\
\midrule
North & \textbf{Niflheim} & Mist, cold, ice \\
South & \textbf{Muspelheim} & Fire, heat, sparks \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

In Niflheim is the spring \textbf{Hvergelmir}, from which flow the icy rivers called \textbf{Elivagar}. Their venom-cold waters harden into ice as they reach the void. The sparks of Muspelheim warm the ice. Where rime and heat meet, life forms.

\subsection{The First Being and the Birth of the Powers}

From the meltwater in the middle of Ginnungagap the first being takes shape.

\begin{itemize}
 \item The frost-giant (\textbf{Ymir}) emerges from the dripping rime. He is the first of the giants (the \textbf{jotnar}). As he sleeps and sweats, more giants are born from him.
 \item The cow \textbf{Audhumla} also forms from the melting rime. Four rivers of milk flow from her and feed Ymir.
 \item Audhumla licks the salty ice-blocks. Over three days she licks free a man named \textbf{Buri}.
\end{itemize}

The line of the powers then descends through several generations.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.4\linewidth}
L{0.45\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Generation} & \textbf{Beings} \\
\midrule
Licked free from the ice & \textbf{Buri} \\
Son of Buri & \textbf{Bor} \\
Bor weds \textbf{Bestla}, a giant's daughter & Their sons \\
Sons of Bor and Bestla & \textbf{Odin}, \textbf{Vili}, \textbf{Ve} \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The gods descend from both the ice-line through Buri and the giant-line through Bestla. Gods and giants share blood from the start.

\subsection{The Slaying and the World from the Body}

The sons of Bor kill Ymir. His blood drowns nearly all the frost-giants. Only \textbf{Bergelmir} and his household escape on a vessel, and from them new giants spring. Then Odin, Vili, and Ve carry Ymir into the middle of Ginnungagap and make the world from him.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.4\linewidth}
L{0.45\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Part of Ymir} & \textbf{Becomes} \\
\midrule
Flesh & The \textbf{earth} \\
Blood & The \textbf{sea} and lakes \\
Bones & The \textbf{mountains} \\
Teeth and broken bones & The \textbf{rocks} and stones \\
Skull & The \textbf{sky} \\
Brains & The \textbf{clouds} \\
Hair & The \textbf{trees} \\
Eyelashes & \textbf{Midgard}, the wall around the world of people \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The gods set four dwarves, \textbf{Nordri}, \textbf{Sudri}, \textbf{Austri}, and \textbf{Vestri}, to hold up the corners of the skull-sky. They take sparks from Muspelheim and set them as sun, moon, and stars, and fix their courses. The maggots in Ymir's flesh become the dwarves.

\subsection{The First Humans}

The gods walk along the seashore and find two pieces of driftwood. From them they make the first humans.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.16\linewidth}
L{0.24\linewidth} L{0.42\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Tree} & \textbf{Becomes} & \textbf{Gift} \\
\midrule
Ash & \textbf{Ask}, the man & Breath and spirit \\
Elm & \textbf{Embla}, the woman & Sense, warmth, blood, and color \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The Voluspa names the three givers as \textbf{Odin}, \textbf{Hoenir}, and \textbf{Lodur}, while Gylfaginning names \textbf{Odin}, \textbf{Vili}, and \textbf{Ve}. The triad is the same shapers under different names.

\subsection{The Nine Worlds and the World-Tree}

The cosmos is organized around the great ash-tree, the world-axis (\textbf{Yggdrasil}). It binds together \textbf{nine worlds}.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.4\linewidth}
L{0.45\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{World} & \textbf{Inhabitants} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Asgard} & The Aesir gods \\
\textbf{Vanaheim} & The Vanir gods \\
\textbf{Alfheim} & The light-elves \\
\textbf{Midgard} & Humans \\
\textbf{Jotunheim} & The giants \\
\textbf{Svartalfheim} & The dark-elves and dwarves \\
\textbf{Muspelheim} & The fire-giants \\
\textbf{Niflheim} & Mist and the dead \\
\textbf{Hel} & The dead under Niflheim \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The number nine is fixed in the verse. The exact membership is reconstructed and varies between sources. Yggdrasil has three great roots reaching three wells. The beings of the tree run the height of the axis.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.3\linewidth}
L{0.55\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Being} & \textbf{Role} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Nidhogg} & The serpent that gnaws the root from below \\
The eagle & Sits in the topmost branches \\
\textbf{Vedrfolnir} & The hawk between the eagle's eyes \\
\textbf{Ratatoskr} & The squirrel who runs the trunk carrying insults \\
Four stags & Browse the branches \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\textbf{Odin} sacrificed an eye at the Well of Mimir to drink from it and gain wisdom. The Norns water the tree daily from the Well of Urd to keep it from rotting.

\subsection{The Two Tribes of Gods}

There are two families of gods. They fought the first war in the world and then made peace, exchanging hostages, after which they are counted as one community.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.16\linewidth}
L{0.32\linewidth} L{0.34\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Tribe} & \textbf{Nature} & \textbf{Members} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Aesir} & Sky, war, sovereignty, law & Odin, Thor, Frigg, Tyr, Heimdall, Baldr \\
\textbf{Vanir} & Fertility, sea, wealth, magic & Njord, Frey, Freyja \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The chief god is the Allfather (\textbf{Odin}), lord of wisdom, war, death, poetry, and magic. \textbf{Thor} is the thunder god and protector of Midgard. \textbf{Frigg} is the queen who knows all fates but speaks none. \textbf{Freyja} is the goddess of love, magic, and the slain. \textbf{Tyr} is the god of law and oaths. \textbf{Heimdall} is the watchman of the gods. \textbf{Loki} is the trickster, a giant among the Aesir, father of monsters. \textbf{Baldr} is the god of light, killed by mistletoe and destined to return.

\subsection{Fate and the Norns}

Above gods and people stands \textbf{fate}. The Old Norse word is \textbf{urdr}, the source of the English word weird. It is impersonal and binding. Even the gods are bound by it. Odin knows he will die at Ragnarok and goes to meet it anyway.

The \textbf{Norns} shape this fate. The three great Norns sit at the Well of Urd.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.24\linewidth}
L{0.36\linewidth} L{0.22\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Norn} & \textbf{Name Means} & \textbf{Tense} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Urd} & That which became & The past \\
\textbf{Verdandi} & That which is becoming & The present \\
\textbf{Skuld} & That which shall be & The future \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

They carve the fates of every person and lay down the laws of life. Lesser norns attend each birth and set its luck.

\subsection{The Afterlives}

There is no single destination for the dead. Where one goes depends on how one dies and which power claims them.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.22\linewidth}
L{0.22\linewidth} L{0.4\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Destination} & \textbf{Ruler} & \textbf{Who Goes There} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Valhalla} & Odin & Half of the battle-slain, chosen by the Valkyries to feast and ready for Ragnarok \\
\textbf{Folkvangr} & Freyja & The other half of the slain \\
\textbf{Hel} & The goddess Hel & Those who die of sickness or old age \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The choosers of the slain (the \textbf{Valkyries}) select who falls in battle and serve in Valhalla.

\subsection{Ragnarok and the Renewed World}

The fate of the gods (\textbf{Ragnarok}) is the foretold end. It is not avoidable. The seeress narrates it as prophecy.

\begin{enumerate}
 \item The age of axes and swords comes. Brother fights brother. The \textbf{Fimbulwinter}, three winters with no summer, falls on the world.
 \item The wolf \textbf{Fenrir} breaks free. The world-serpent \textbf{Jormungandr} rises from the sea. The ship \textbf{Naglfar} sails with the dead.
 \item \textbf{Surt} leads the fire-giants across the bridge \textbf{Bifrost}, which shatters. The gods and the slain of Valhalla march to the plain \textbf{Vigrid}.
\end{enumerate}

The powers and their foes destroy one another.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.24\linewidth}
L{0.26\linewidth} L{0.34\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{God} & \textbf{Foe} & \textbf{Outcome} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Odin} & \textbf{Fenrir} & Odin is devoured \\
\textbf{Vidar} & \textbf{Fenrir} & Vidar avenges Odin and kills the wolf \\
\textbf{Thor} & \textbf{Jormungandr} & Both die \\
\textbf{Frey} & \textbf{Surt} & Frey falls \\
\textbf{Tyr} & \textbf{Garm} & Both die \\
\textbf{Heimdall} & \textbf{Loki} & Both die \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

Surt flings fire over the whole world. The earth sinks into the sea and the stars fall. Then the earth rises again, green and new, from the sea, and crops grow unsown. \textbf{Vidar} and \textbf{Vali} live, \textbf{Modi} and \textbf{Magni} keep Thor's hammer, and \textbf{Baldr} returns from Hel. Two humans, \textbf{Lif} and \textbf{Lifthrasir}, hid in a wood and survive to repeople the world. A new hall, brighter than the sun, rises. The renewed world begins.

\subsection{Comparison}

The Norse model follows the base shape closely. A void poised between two opposite poles gives the first being, ordering powers shape the world from its body, and a binding law stands over the whole. The one distinct feature is that the order is built to fall and rise again, so renewal is cyclical rather than final. The pattern holds throughout. \textbf{Undifferentiated potential differentiates into a structured world held together by a law that even its makers must obey.}
