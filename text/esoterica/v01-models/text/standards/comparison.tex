\section{The Six Traditions in Comparison}

This collection treated two systems in the deepest detail, \textbf{Buddhism} and \textbf{Kabbalah}. They serve as the base models. The six traditions gathered in this chapter, \textbf{Hinduism}, esoteric \textbf{Christianity}, esoteric \textbf{Islam}, \textbf{Sikhism}, \textbf{Taoism}, and \textbf{Hermeticism}, are living faiths, each with its own layered picture of the cosmos and the self. Set beside the two base models they show one shared pattern. A source beyond name. A stirring that breaks the one into the many. A layered self with a divine core. And a return of the many back to the one.

\subsection{The Common Threads}

\textbf{Emanation and return.} Every system runs the same two-stroke motion. The one goes out into the many, then the many return home. Taoism counts it one to two to three to the ten thousand things, then back by effortless action. Kabbalah pours the Infinite through the sefirot and four worlds, then gathers the scattered sparks. Hermeticism descends the Light through the spheres into the fall, then climbs the soul back gate by gate. The names differ. The shape does not.

\textbf{The inner divine spark.} Each tradition places a piece of the source inside the self. The atman of Hinduism, the divine spark of Kabbalah, the buddha-nature of Buddhism, the indwelling spirit of Christianity, the secret heart of Sufism, the soul-spark of Sikhism, the spirit drawn from the three treasures in Taoism, and the archetypal Human within in Hermeticism. The self is not cut off from the source. It carries the source as its own deepest layer.

\textbf{The ascent.} Because the core is divine, the return is a climb. Hermeticism states this most explicitly, the soul rising through seven spheres and shedding a vice at each. Kabbalah climbs the four worlds. Taoist inner alchemy refines essence to breath to spirit and then to emptiness. Sufism passes through stations to annihilation. The path is layered, and the self rises through the layers it once descended.

\textbf{The oneness.} At the top every ladder ends in the same place, a single reality beyond name. Brahman, Ein Sof, the Tao, the One, Ik Onkar, emptiness, Allah, the All. The many are not finally separate. They are the one in many forms, and the goal is to recognize that.

\subsection{The Genuine Differences}

The systems are not identical, and the differences are real.

\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Personal or impersonal source.} Sufism, Christianity, and Sikhism address the ultimate as a personal beloved or sovereign. Buddhism, the Tao, and Brahman lean toward an impersonal ground. Hermeticism and Kabbalah sit between, a Mind and an Infinite that are neither quite person nor quite thing.
 \item \textbf{The status of the self at the end.} Hinduism and Sufism can dissolve the self fully into the source. Christianity and Sikhism more often keep a relation between the soul and its God even in union. Buddhism denies a fixed self throughout.
 \item \textbf{The world as real or as veil.} Hinduism calls the many a veil of maya. Taoism and the Hermetic and Kabbalistic systems treat the unfolded world as a genuine, if lower, expression of the source rather than a mere illusion.
 \item \textbf{The engine of the fall.} Hermeticism makes the descent a fall through love of one's own reflection. Kabbalah makes it a shattering of vessels. Taoism makes it a natural unfolding with no fall at all. The cost of being many is told very differently.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Universal Theme}

Set the eight together and one pattern stands out. \textbf{Everything goes out from a single source and returns to it.} The source pours itself into a layered cosmos and a layered self. The self forgets, descends, and is bound. Then it remembers, climbs, and returns. This go-out-and-return motion is the one model behind the many. The six traditions and the two base models are eight tellings of that single pattern.
