\chapter{The One Model Behind the Many}

This chapter states the generalized model that the traditions appear to share. It is offered as a working
hypothesis, the simplest pattern that fits the surveyed material, not as a doctrine. The claim is modest in
form and large in scope. Strip each tradition of its particular vocabulary and imagery, and a single
sequence shows through almost everywhere.

\section{The Seven Movements}

The shared pattern can be set out as seven movements. They are stages of one process, not separate events,
and a given tradition may compress several into one or dwell on a single movement for its whole literature.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Source.} There is one origin, beyond name, number, and division. It is not yet a world
  and not yet a self. It is full, undivided, and complete.
  \item \textbf{The First Move.} Something stirs. The traditions describe it as a will to give, a wish to be
  known, an out-breath, or a withdrawal that opens room. With it, the bare possibility of an other appears.
  \item \textbf{The Descent.} The one becomes many. Reality unfolds in graded levels, from the most subtle
  near the source to the most dense and divided. This is emanation, the pouring-out, the chain of worlds.
  \item \textbf{The Forgetting.} In the descent the many lose sight of the one. The traditions call it a
  veiling, a shattering, a fall, a sleep, or simple ignorance. Separateness feels total and the source seems
  absent.
  \item \textbf{The Human Turn.} The human being stands at the meeting point of the levels, dense like the
  world yet carrying a spark of the source. This makes the human the place where the process can reverse.
  \item \textbf{The Return.} The path. A staged movement back toward the source, by remembering, purifying,
  ascending, gathering, or waking. It commonly runs through purification, then illumination, then union.
  \item \textbf{The Reunion.} The many are gathered back. The traditions name it liberation, union, repair,
  awakening, deification, or homecoming. The separateness that felt total is seen through, and the source is
  found to have been present all along.
\end{enumerate}

The fit is not forced and it is not perfect. Buddhism has no source that creates, so its first two rows
describe a beginningless condition rather than an act. Taoism resists the language of fall and return and
prefers balance. These are real and noted. Yet even these cases bend toward the same shape. Buddhism still
has a movement from ignorance into the apparent worlds and a movement back to awakening. Taoism still has a
going-out from the simple to the many and a returning to the source. The pattern holds across the widest
spread of cultures the survey could gather.

\section{The Thing Behind the Thing}

The deeper claim is that the traditions are not only sharing a narrative shape. They are pointing, from many
angles, at one reality. Each had partial access to it, through contemplation, vision, reasoning, and long
practice, and each reported it in the terms its culture made available. The convergence of the maps is the
evidence that there is a single territory.

What is that reality. The traditions agree on its marks more than on its name. It is one, prior to all
division. It is the ground of both the world and the self, so that to go deep enough into either is to find
it. It is not an object among objects and cannot be reached by the senses or fixed by a concept, which is why
the accounts so often turn to negation. And it is the source of a pull, felt as the wish to return, to
remember, to come home.

In the language of the wider research program that this survey serves, this is the flow behind the field, the
one source from which the many forms arise and to which they return, the way that lies beyond all the senses.
The traditions are early and independent attempts to chart it. They are worth studying closely because they
have already mapped, in their own terms, the structures that any complete account of consciousness, the
inner life, and the divine will have to address.

\section{On the Differences}

The differences remain, and a few are worth keeping as alternatives rather than errors. The dual-root model
of the Zoroastrian and Gnostic traditions takes the problem of evil more seriously than the systems that
treat darkness as mere privation. The cyclic and pluralist time of the Indic and Mesoamerican traditions
holds open possibilities that a single bounded history closes. The no-creator stance of Buddhism is a
genuine fork, a reminder that the first two movements may describe a beginningless condition rather than an
origin. These widen the picture. They do not break the shared shape, which is the going-out of the one into
the many and the finding of the way back.
