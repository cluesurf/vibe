\chapter{The Common Threads}

The traditions have now been surveyed one by one. The aim of this part is to set their models beside each
other and read across them. The first job is descriptive, to lay out where the models agree and where they
diverge. The deeper job, taken up in the next chapter, is to ask what single reality they may all be
describing.

A note on method. The differences are recorded, but they are not the point. No model is complete, and most
of the variation turns out to be a matter of emphasis, vocabulary, and which stage of one process a tradition
chooses to dwell on. What follows foregrounds the structures that recur.

\section{The Families of God-Definitions}

The single most important finding is that the traditions do not define the ultimate in one way. They define
it in a handful of recurring ways, and a single tradition often holds more than one at once. Six families
cover almost every case.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.30\linewidth} L{0.60\linewidth}}
\caption{Six recurring families of how the ultimate is defined.}\\
\toprule
\textbf{Family} & \textbf{Where it appears} \\
\midrule
The impersonal ground & The absolute (Brahman), the way (the Tao), the Infinite (Ein Sof), emptiness and the dharmakaya in Buddhism \\
The personal creator & The Abrahamic God, the craftsman (Ptah), the creator who rose from the lake (Viracocha), the storm-king (Marduk) \\
The cosmic mind & The divine Mind (Nous) of Hermeticism and Neoplatonism, the speaking Word (Logos) \\
The generative process & The way (the Tao), the primal generator (Rod), the cosmic egg, the pairing of the two poles \\
The void or absence & The no-creator of Buddhism, the uncertainty of the oldest Hindu creation hymn \\
The dual principle & The wise light and the destructive dark held as two roots in Zoroastrian thought \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

Two patterns sit across these families. The first is a high god who withdraws after creating and leaves the
running of the world to lesser powers, common in the indigenous traditions and the older pantheons. The
second is emanation, the production of the many from the one in graded steps, which is the explicit method of
Kabbalah, Neoplatonism, Hermeticism, and the Gnostic systems.

\section{The Shapes of Creation}

Creation is told through a small set of moves. A given account usually combines several of them.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{From a one.} The source overflows or emanates, and the many descend from it in stages. This
  is the dominant esoteric pattern.
  \item \textbf{From the waters or the void.} A formless deep or an empty darkness stirs and yields form.
  \item \textbf{By a word.} The cosmos is spoken, named, or sung into being.
  \item \textbf{By a sacrifice.} A first being is divided, and the parts of the world are made from the body.
  \item \textbf{By a separation.} Sky is parted from earth, light from dark, the waters above from the waters
  below.
  \item \textbf{By a struggle.} Order is won by overcoming a chaos-power, often a serpent or a dragon of the
  sea.
\end{itemize}

The esoteric traditions favor emanation and the word. The older and more anthropomorphic cosmologies favor
separation, sacrifice, and struggle. They are not opposed accounts so much as the same descent told at
different levels of abstraction.

\section{The Architecture of Reality}

The traditions build the cosmos out of a few recurring structures.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A tree or axis} joining the levels of the world, from the world-ash of the north to the Tree
  of Life of Kabbalah to the inverted cosmic tree of the Hindu texts.
  \item \textbf{A central mountain} or a navel of the world marking the axis.
  \item \textbf{A ladder of worlds}, stacked heavens and underworlds through which the soul rises or falls.
  \item \textbf{A wheel} of time or of rebirth.
  \item \textbf{A subtle body}, an inner anatomy of channels and centers that mirrors the cosmos.
  \item \textbf{A correspondence} between the small and the large, the human and the universe, summed up in
  the formula that what is above is as what is below.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Shape of Time}

Two pictures of time divide the traditions cleanly.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.22\linewidth} L{0.34\linewidth} L{0.34\linewidth}}
\caption{The two pictures of cosmic time.}\\
\toprule
 & \textbf{Linear and bounded} & \textbf{Cyclic and vast} \\
\midrule
Shape & A single arc from a beginning to an end & Repeating ages without a first or last \\
Examples & Zoroastrian, Jewish, Christian, Islamic & Hindu, Buddhist, Maya, Aztec, Stoic \\
Worlds & Usually one world & Many worlds, in succession or at once \\
End & A final renovation or judgment & Dissolution and remaking, over and over \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The cyclic traditions tend to immense durations and to a plurality of worlds. The bounded traditions tend to
a single creation with a purpose that completes. Even here the two converge at the edges, since the bounded
traditions place a renewal after the end and the cyclic ones grant each cycle its own integrity.

\section{Light and Dark, Good and Evil}

The traditions split on whether reality runs on a contest of two powers.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.30\linewidth} L{0.60\linewidth}}
\caption{How the traditions place evil and the dark.}\\
\toprule
\textbf{Model of evil} & \textbf{Where it appears} \\
\midrule
A cosmic dualism of two roots & Zoroastrian light against dark, the Gnostic spirit against matter \\
A consequence or a broken structure & The other side and the shells of Kabbalah, fallen light in the wrong place \\
Ignorance, an inner not a cosmic enemy & The root unknowing of Buddhism, with no evil god, only obstacles \\
A complementary polarity & The two poles of Taoist thought, which balance rather than war \\
A force of disorder against order & The chaos-serpent and the disorder against cosmic order in the old pantheons \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

Only a minority make darkness a true co-equal power. Most treat it as a privation, a consequence, an
ignorance, or a polarity. Light, by contrast, is almost everywhere the first thing, the divine substance, the
medium of both creation and knowledge.

\section{The Self and the Path}

The inner teachings agree more than the outer myths. Three points recur across nearly every tradition.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A layered self with a divine core.} The person is built in levels, a body, a life, a mind,
  and an innermost self or spark that is continuous with the source.
  \item \textbf{A fall and a return.} The soul has descended, forgotten, or been scattered, and the work of a
  life is to remember, to ascend, or to be gathered back.
  \item \textbf{A graded path.} The return is staged, a ladder of levels, stations, or grounds, often summed
  as a movement through purification to illumination to union.
\end{itemize}

These three are the bridge to the next chapter. The outer cosmologies differ widely, but the inner accounts
of what a person is and what a person is for converge on a single shape.
