\section{The Aggregates and the Eight Consciousnesses}

Buddhist Abhidharma takes the person apart. Where common sense finds a self, the analysis finds only a bundle of factors, sorted and re-sorted by different schemes. The base classification is the \textbf{five aggregates}. From it grow the sense-bases, the elements, the long list of mental factors, and, in Yogachara, the \textbf{eight consciousnesses}. No self is found among any of them.

\subsection{The Five Aggregates}

A person reduces to \textbf{five aggregates} (skandha, Tibetan phung po), five heaps that exhaust what a being is.

\begin{longtable}{@{}
rL{2.6cm}L{2.6cm}L{4.8cm}@{}}
\toprule
\# & Sanskrit & English & Gloss \\
\midrule
1 & rupa & form & matter and the body \\
2 & vedana & feeling & the hedonic tone, pleasant, unpleasant, neutral \\
3 & samjna & perception & recognition, grasping the mark of an object \\
4 & samskara & formations & the volitional and constructing factors \\
5 & vijnana & consciousness & bare awareness at a sense-door \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The first aggregate, \textbf{rupa}, is the form or material side. The other four are the \textbf{nama}, the name or mental side. Together they make nama-rupa, name-and-form.

\subsection{The Sense-Bases and Elements}

The same factors are re-sorted by the doors through which experience enters. This gives the \textbf{twelve sense-bases} (ayatana). They are six inner faculties and six outer object-classes. The six faculties are eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and \textbf{mind}. The mind counts as a sixth sense, and its objects are dharmas, mental phenomena. The six objects are visible form, sound, smell, taste, the tangible, and mental objects.

Add the six \textbf{consciousnesses} that arise when a faculty meets its object, and the count rises to the \textbf{eighteen elements} (dhatu). Six faculties, six objects, and six consciousnesses. Each act of knowing needs all three. A faculty, an object, and a consciousness. Their meeting is contact (sparsa). The aggregate of consciousness expands into the six consciousnesses of this scheme.

\subsection{The Fifty-One Mental Factors}

The fourth aggregate, formations, unfolds into a detailed list of \textbf{fifty-one mental factors} (chaitasika), the mental events that accompany a primary mind. This is the list of Asanga's Abhidharmasamuccaya, standard across Tibetan Buddhism. It falls into six groups.

\begin{longtable}{@{}
L{6.2cm}r@{}}
\toprule
Group & Count \\
\midrule
Omnipresent (sarvatraga) & 5 \\
Object-ascertaining (viniyata) & 5 \\
Virtuous (kusala) & 11 \\
Root afflictions (mula-klesa) & 6 \\
Secondary afflictions (upaklesa) & 20 \\
Changeable (aniyata) & 4 \\
\textbf{Total} & \textbf{51} \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The five \textbf{omnipresent} factors are present in every moment of mind. They are contact, feeling, perception, intention, and attention. So feeling and perception, which are full aggregates of their own, also appear here as the first two omnipresent factors. The six \textbf{root afflictions} include the three poisons, attachment, aversion, and ignorance, plus pride, doubt, and afflicted view. The eleven \textbf{virtuous} factors are their counterweights, headed by the three wholesome roots of non-attachment, non-hatred, and non-ignorance.

\subsection{The Eight Consciousnesses}

Early Abhidharma stops at six consciousnesses. \textbf{Yogachara} (Cittamatra, mind-only) adds two more, giving the \textbf{eight consciousnesses} (ashta-vijnana).

\begin{longtable}{@{}
rL{2.8cm}L{2.8cm}L{4.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\# & Sanskrit & English & Function \\
\midrule
1 & caksur-vijnana & eye-consciousness & bare seeing \\
2 & srotra-vijnana & ear-consciousness & bare hearing \\
3 & ghrana-vijnana & nose-consciousness & bare smelling \\
4 & jihva-vijnana & tongue-consciousness & bare tasting \\
5 & kaya-vijnana & body-consciousness & bare touching \\
6 & mano-vijnana & mental consciousness & thinking, judging, integrating the senses \\
7 & klishta-manas & afflicted mind & self-grasping, the I-maker \\
8 & alaya-vijnana & storehouse consciousness & the substrate holding all seeds \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The first six are the familiar awarenesses. The \textbf{seventh}, the afflicted mind, sits below the surface and continually mistakes the eighth for a permanent self, coloring all thought with I and mine. The \textbf{eighth}, the \textbf{storehouse consciousness} (alaya-vijnana, Tibetan kun gzhi, the all-base), is the deepest layer. It stores the seeds (bija) of all past actions and ripens them into future experience. It is subliminal, continuous, and neutral, and it carries forward between lives. It is not a self, but its continuity is what the seventh mistakes for one.

The flow runs both ways. Actions plant seeds down into the storehouse, called perfuming (vasana). The seeds ripen and project the next moment of experience, including the apparent outer world. This is the mind-only claim. The world we see is the storehouse's seeds maturing, not external substance.

\subsection{The Three Natures}

Yogachara reads reality through \textbf{three natures} (trisvabhava). The \textbf{imputed} (parikalpita) is the wholly fabricated, the names and the false split of subject and object projected onto experience. The \textbf{dependent} (paratantra) is the flow of dependently-arisen mental events, real as process but not as it appears. The \textbf{perfected} (parinishpanna) is the dependent nature emptied of the imputed, things as they truly are, suchness. The standard image is a rope mistaken for a snake in dim light. The absent snake is the imputed. The rope is the dependent. Seeing the rope as merely rope, free of the snake, is the perfected. At buddhahood the eight consciousnesses turn about and become the pure wisdoms, the defiled mind transformed into clear knowing.
