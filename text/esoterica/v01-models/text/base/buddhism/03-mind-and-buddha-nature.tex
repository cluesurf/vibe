\section{Mind, the Ground, and Buddha-Nature}

If emptiness is the view of how things lack solidity, this is the view of what mind is at its base. Tibetan Buddhism teaches that the deepest nature of mind is already pure and awake. The path is uncovering, not manufacturing. This section covers the concepts behind that claim and the debate over how to read it.

\subsection*{Buddha-nature}

\textbf{Buddha-nature} (\textbf{tathagatagarbha}) is the teaching that all sentient beings possess the seed or essence of buddhahood. The standard image is the sun. The mind is like the sun, always shining, with the afflictions like passing clouds. The clouds obscure but do not change the sun. Awakening is the clouds clearing, not a new sun being made. The key Indian source is the \textbf{Uttaratantra}, attributed to Maitreya through Asanga, which lists the qualities of the buddha-element present in all beings.

The question is what this buddha-nature is. Is it just emptiness, the mere absence of inherent existence in mind? Or is it a real luminous presence, full of qualities? This question splits Tibetan scholarship into two camps.

\subsection*{Self-emptiness versus other-emptiness}

This is one of the most important live debates in Tibetan Buddhism.

\textbf{Self-emptiness} (\textbf{rangtong}) is the first view. Phenomena, including mind and buddha-nature, are empty of their own inherent existence. Emptiness here is a sheer absence. It denies own-being and asserts nothing positive in its place. Buddha-nature is read as the emptiness of mind, the potential created by that openness.

\textbf{Other-emptiness} (\textbf{shentong}) is the second view. The ultimate, the buddha-nature, is empty of everything other than itself, empty of the passing defilements, but it is not empty of its own luminous awakened nature. It is a real, present, self-aware luminosity, full of buddha-qualities, empty only of the foreign stains laid over it.

The contrast in one line each.

\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Rangtong}. The ultimate is empty of itself. Emptiness is the sheer absence of inherent existence.
 \item \textbf{Shentong}. The ultimate is empty of other. A luminous awareness remains, empty only of stains.
\end{itemize}

The \textbf{Jonang} school is the home of shentong, systematized by Dolpopa Sherab Gyaltsen in the fourteenth century. Dolpopa held that the ultimate buddha-nature is a permanent, stable, eternal ground, truly existent in its own way, distinct from the conventional phenomena which are self-empty. The \textbf{Gelug} school, following Tsongkhapa, rejected shentong sharply. Gelug holds strict self-emptiness Prasangika. To them a truly existent ultimate sounds like the very eternalism Madhyamaka was built to refute. The Kagyu and Nyingma schools are often sympathetic to shentong, especially in their meditation and tantra teachings. The Sakya tend toward self-emptiness with their own subtle readings. This is a real disagreement, not a settled point. Both sides cite the same scriptures and reach different conclusions.

\subsection*{The all-ground versus pure awareness}

There is a parallel question at the level of consciousness. What is the deepest layer of mind?

The \textbf{all-ground consciousness} or store-consciousness (\textbf{alaya}) is taught in the Yogachara system. It is the basal layer of mind that carries karmic seeds and imprints from one moment and one life to the next. It is the substrate of ordinary cyclic continuity. In Dzogchen the all-ground is still a deluded, conditioned state. It is the storehouse of confusion, neutral and dim, not yet awakened.

\textbf{Pure awareness} (\textbf{rigpa}) is the knowing, awake nature of mind itself. Rigpa is not the all-ground. Rigpa is the recognition of the true nature, free of grasping, self-aware, primordially pure. The whole of Dzogchen turns on distinguishing pure awareness from the ordinary mind and from the all-ground.

\subsection*{Ordinary mind versus the nature of mind}

Tibetan makes a careful distinction in vocabulary. \textbf{Ordinary mind} is the discursive, grasping, dualistic mind that thinks and reacts. It is the mover of cyclic existence. The \textbf{nature of mind} is the naked, empty, luminous ground of that same mind. It is the same mind, seen at its source. Thoughts arise from and dissolve back into the nature of mind like waves on water. The waves are not other than the water. To recognize the nature of mind is the point of the practice.

\subsection*{The Dzogchen Ground}

The \textbf{Great Perfection} (\textbf{Dzogchen}) is the highest teaching of the Nyingma school. Its central concept is the \textbf{Ground}, the primordial basis of everything, of both cyclic existence and liberation. The Ground has two inseparable aspects.

\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Primordial purity} (\textbf{kadag}). The Ground is empty, pure from the very beginning, never stained, never needing to be cleansed. This is the emptiness aspect.
 \item \textbf{Spontaneous presence} (\textbf{lhundrub}). The Ground spontaneously and effortlessly displays appearances and qualities. This is the luminous, manifesting aspect. Nothing is fabricated. All arises by itself.
\end{itemize}

Primordial purity and spontaneous presence are not two things. They are emptiness and luminosity as one, the two faces of the single Ground.

\subsection*{Clear light and luminosity}

The cognizant quality of the Ground is named the \textbf{clear light}. This does not mean visible light like a lamp. It means the luminous, knowing, aware quality of mind, the capacity to cognize, to be lucid, to illuminate experience. Mind is described as having two inseparable qualities at once.

\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Empty}. No inherent existence, no findable thing.
 \item \textbf{Luminous} or cognizant. Aware, knowing, clear, never blank.
\end{itemize}

The union of empty and luminous is the nature of mind. Empty, so it can be anything. Luminous, so it knows. This clear light is the most subtle level of mind, said to be present even in deep sleep and at death. It is the very thing recognized as buddha-nature in the Dzogchen and tantra traditions.

So the picture coheres. Buddha-nature, the nature of mind, pure awareness, the Ground, and the clear light are different names and framings for the one awake nature that practice aims to recognize. The schools differ on how positively to describe it. They agree that the nature is already there to be found.
