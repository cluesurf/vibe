\section{The Infinite (Ein Sof)}

The same opening is now restated in the tradition's own vocabulary. What the plain
story called the source, Kabbalah calls \textbf{Ein Sof}.

\subsection{The Two Registers of the Ultimate}

Kabbalah defines the ultimate \textbf{twice}, on two registers that never collapse
into one.

The first register is the \textbf{unknowable Infinite}, Ein Sof. It is the Godhead
before all name, thought, number, and relation. It is not a being. Nothing positive
can be said of it. Every name is a limit, and the Infinite has no limit.

The second register is the \textbf{knowable ten-fold structure}. From the Infinite
emanate the \textbf{ten emanations} (sefirot), the divine attributes through which
the unknowable acts and becomes, in some measure, visible. The sefirot are not the Infinite. They
are its channels. The two registers meet in the creation account, where the
unknowable Infinite achieves its purpose through the knowable structure.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.18\linewidth} L{0.34\linewidth} L{0.34\linewidth}}
\caption{The two registers of the ultimate in Kabbalah.}\\
\toprule
 & \textbf{First register} & \textbf{Second register} \\
\midrule
Name & The Infinite (Ein Sof) & The ten sefirot \\
Status & Unknowable, attributeless & Knowable structure \\
Relation & The hidden source & The revealed channels \\
Can be named & No & Yes, by attribute \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\subsection{Ein Sof, Without End}

\textbf{Ein Sof} means \enquote{without end} or \enquote{no limit.} The phrase is a
negation. It states what God is not. There is no end, no boundary, no measure. It is
the Godhead before any emanation, before the sefirot, before the worlds, before
time.

It cannot be named, because a name is a limit. It cannot be grasped by thought,
because thought divides and bounds, and the Infinite has no edge to hold. It is
simple and undivided, an absolute oneness with no parts and no distinctions.

The reasoning is structural, not merely devotional.

\begin{itemize}
 \item A \textbf{name} singles out. To name a thing is to mark it off from what it
 is not. Ein Sof has no \enquote{not.} It is all there is at the root. So no
 name fits.
 \item A \textbf{thought} grasps a form, and forms have edges. The Infinite has no
 edge. The mind reaching for it finds nothing to hold.
 \item Any \textbf{attribute} is a limit. \enquote{Wise} excludes \enquote{not
 wise.} \enquote{Powerful} sits on a scale. The Infinite is prior to every
 scale.
\end{itemize}

This is why the tradition proceeds through the sefirot. The sefirot are not Ein Sof.
They are the channels through which the unknowable becomes, in some measure,
knowable. The Infinite stays hidden. Its effects become revealed. The term Ein Sof
is itself relatively late. It is the technical name the tradition gave to the
unbounded God in the Kabbalah of thirteenth-century Provence and Gerona.

\subsection{Atzmut and Ein Sof}

Later Kabbalah, especially Hasidic thought, draws a careful distinction between two
words for the source.

\textbf{Atzmut} means \enquote{essence} or \enquote{selfhood.} It is the divine
essence as it is in itself. In this refined usage, \textbf{Ein Sof} is the first
expression of that essence, its infinite light or infinite capacity, but not the
bare essence itself.

\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Atzmut} is the essence. It is beyond even the category of
 \enquote{infinite,} since to call it infinite is already to describe it.
 \item \textbf{Ein Sof} points to that essence under the aspect of limitlessness.
 It is the closest the mind can come, and it is still infinitely far.
\end{itemize}

Not every school draws this distinction. In many classical texts Ein Sof simply is
the hidden Godhead with no further split. The Atzmut distinction is most developed
in Chabad Hasidic philosophy. Treat it as school-dependent vocabulary.

\subsection{Ein Sof Or, the Infinite Light}

The \textbf{light of the Infinite} (Ein Sof Or), often written Or Ein Sof,
is the first concept that lets infinity relate to creation.

Light (ohr) is the standard Kabbalistic metaphor for divine emanation. It
is what radiates from the source without diminishing the source, the way one candle
lights another. The infinite light filled all reality before creation. There was no
\enquote{place} that was not full of undifferentiated divine light. This total
fullness is the starting condition of the creation story, and it is the light
that the contraction later withdraws.

The order is essence, then light, then everything the light builds.

\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Ein Sof} is the source.
 \item \textbf{Or Ein Sof} is its radiance, its infinite outpouring.
 \item Light is not a thing God makes. It is God's own presence expressed outward.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sovev and Memale, Transcendence and Immanence}

Two phrases describe how the Infinite relates to the worlds. They are a central pair
in Hasidic cosmology.

\textbf{Encompassing all worlds} (Sovev kol almin) is
\textbf{transcendence}. This is the divine light that surrounds and contains all
worlds equally. It is not graded. It does not enter the worlds as an inner force. To
the encompassing light, a vast world and a small world are the same, because the
Infinite is not measured by them.

\textbf{Filling all worlds} (Memale kol almin) is \textbf{immanence}. This
is the divine light that enters each world, adapted to its level. It is the inner
life-force of each created thing. It is graded. It clothes itself differently in a
high world than in a low one.

\begin{longtable}{L{0.16\linewidth} L{0.36\linewidth} L{0.36\linewidth}}
\caption{Transcendence and immanence as two lights of the Infinite.}\\
\toprule
 & \textbf{Sovev kol almin} & \textbf{Memale kol almin} \\
\midrule
Meaning & Encompassing all worlds & Filling all worlds \\
Aspect & Transcendence & Immanence \\
Form & Undifferentiated, beyond & Differentiated, within \\
Role & Surrounds the worlds & Inner soul of the worlds \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

Both are lights, not the essence. Both come from Ein Sof. The teaching is that God
is at once wholly beyond creation and wholly within it, and the essence is beyond
even this distinction.

\subsection{Nistar and Nigleh, Hidden and Revealed}

The \textbf{hidden} (nistar) and the \textbf{revealed} (nigleh) name
the two faces of divinity and of Torah.

\begin{itemize}
 \item The \textbf{hidden} (nistar) is the concealed dimension. Ein Sof, the inner
 reasons, the esoteric. Kabbalah itself is called the hidden Torah.
 \item The \textbf{revealed} (nigleh) is the revealed dimension. The sefirot as they
 act, the visible law, the exoteric.
\end{itemize}

Reality is built so that the hidden source clothes itself in revealed garments. The
garments both conceal and reveal. They hide the Infinite, since you see the world
and not God. And they reveal it, since the world is how the Infinite shows itself.
Concealment is itself a form of disclosure.

\subsection{Bittul, Self-Nullification}

\textbf{Self-nullification} (bittul), also rendered self-effacement, is the proper
stance of a created being before the Infinite. If only Ein Sof truly is, then a
creature's sense of standalone existence is, at the deepest level, an illusion.
Bittul is the recognition and the felt experience of this. The self quiets its own
claim to independent reality and becomes transparent to the source.

\begin{itemize}
 \item The \textbf{nullification of somethingness} (bittul hayesh) is the lower
 grade. The self exists but submits, humbling its ego before God.
 \item The \textbf{nullification in existence} (bittul bimetziut) is the higher
 grade. The self does not experience itself as separate at all, like a ray
 within the sun that has no identity apart from it.
\end{itemize}

Bittul is the experiential counterpart to the metaphysics. If Ein Sof is the only
true existence, bittul is the inner experience of knowing that.
