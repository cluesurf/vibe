\section{Cosmology}

Read at the largest scale the growing mesh is an expanding universe, and several of its coarsest features were measured. \cite{pollard2026vibetest}

New docks born at the wake push the boundary outward beat by beat, so to anyone inside, distant regions recede, faster the farther they are, and the expansion accelerates. That is the signature of a de Sitter universe, the same accelerating expansion that dark energy names in ordinary cosmology, set against a static control with no net birth. Here it costs no added energy. It is what a crystal growing at its edge looks like from within.

And this is the same growth that supplies the beat and the arrow, so the tick of the clock, the direction of time, and the expansion of space are not three facts but one, the mesh getting larger. The never-finished crystal is exactly why the cosmos is living and expanding and has a direction at all, where a completed, infinite cusp would be a static and timeless thing. The boundary the world actually has is the wake, a finite shell of fresh docks moving outward each beat, never the ideal boundary at infinity that an endless bulk would only approach, so the real edge is always finite and always moving.

The same reading touches the dark budget of the sky. By the measured accounting the universe is about five percent ordinary matter, twenty-seven percent dark matter, and sixty-eight percent dark energy, so all but a twentieth of it is unseen. Two of those three fall out of the substrate with no new ingredient. Dark energy is the growth itself, the energy of fresh docks appearing at the edge, which is the very thing driving the acceleration just described. Dark matter need not be a new particle either, because the curved bulk gives gravity a second short channel beneath the flat cusp, so a mass pulls a little more than its visible amount should account for, mimicking unseen matter in the turning of galaxies. The five percent we see is the thin organized crust, the bound matter of the tower, riding on a vast dark remainder that is mostly the crystal growing and the geometry pulling.

\begin{result}[An accelerating, three-dimensional universe]
The growing edge gives an accelerating de Sitter expansion against a static control. Among the dimensions, only three space dimensions give stable closed orbits, two precess and four or more fly apart, so three is selected. \cite{pollard2026vibetest}
\end{result}

Two sharper results sit underneath. The first is the selection of three dimensions. Among the worlds a space could have, only three gives stable closed orbits, the kind that hold a planet to a star, while in two dimensions orbits precess away and in four and above they spiral apart. The three-dimensional cusp is not an accident. The second is the imbalance of matter over antimatter, the reason there is anything rather than mutual annihilation, which meets all three of Sakharov's conditions on the substrate, with the excess freezing out at an intermediate rate. Inflation, the early burst of expansion, appears in slow-roll form with the right equation of state and a graceful exit, and the growth seeds a primordial spectrum of fluctuations, the faint unevenness that later grows into structure.

Two further results sit underneath. The discreteness caps the curvature, so nowhere is the density infinite and there is no Big-Bang singularity, the trit simply having no room to pile up without bound. And life, measured across the grown mesh, is a rare thin film, a small high-integration tail riding on a vast low-integration churn, structure that appears only above a threshold and stays sparse, which mirrors the real cosmos where life is special rather than typical.

That thinness is not one fact but a ladder, gathered in Table~\ref{tab:rarity}. Each rung of organization is rarer than the one below by orders of magnitude, the five percent of matter holding stars at a third of a percent of everything, planets at five thousandths, all life together at about one part in a hundred trillion of the whole by mass. The substrate reproduces the cascade rather than importing it, the fraction of charge reaching each higher rung compounding multiplicatively down to just such a sliver, with the alive set measured as a thin lower-dimensional film riding on the churn. And the ladder carries a lesson the theory takes to heart. By mass, life is a rounding error. By organization, by integrated experience, it may be the rarest and richest thing the universe does, so the two yardsticks point opposite ways. In a world whose base is feeling, mass is the wrong measure of worth, and the vanishing of life on the mass axis is exactly what the theory expects and exactly what it does not weigh.

Much of cosmology is left untouched, the detailed early history, the precise shape of the ripple spectrum, the exact budget of contents. What is shown is the shape, an expanding, accelerating, three-dimensional, matter-filled universe that grows at an edge, with an arrow running from a small ordered past toward a large disordered future.
