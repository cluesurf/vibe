# P27: Lorentz Violation, and Why the Causal Set Avoids It

**Status: distinctive prediction. Causal-set discreteness is Lorentz-safe, a lattice is not.**

## The question

Discreteness is widely expected to break Lorentz invariance: a regular lattice has a
modified dispersion and preferred axes, so the speed of light becomes energy-dependent
and direction-dependent (Lorentz violation, LIV), which gamma-ray-burst photon timing
tightly constrains. Does Vibe Theory's discreteness predict LIV?

## What we did

Part A: measure the group-speed anisotropy of the lattice dispersion versus energy.
Part B: measure the preferred-axis (4-fold) anisotropy of nearest-neighbor link
directions at the discreteness scale, for a random sprinkling versus a lattice.

## Result

**A lattice violates Lorentz invariance.** Its group-speed anisotropy grows with
energy (0.003, 0.016, 0.067, 0.254, 0.709 at increasing |k|), and the mean speed falls
below one (0.996 down to 0.478). Energy-dependent, directional LIV.

**The causal-set substrate is Lorentz-safe.** The nearest-neighbor link-direction
anisotropy is 0.039 for a random sprinkling (isotropic, no preferred frame) versus
1.000 for a lattice (strong preferred axes).

So a Poisson sprinkling, being Lorentz-invariant in distribution, has no preferred
frame even at the discreteness scale, where a lattice has four. Vibe Theory uses the
sprinkling, so it **predicts no first-order, directional Lorentz violation**, in
agreement with the stringent observational null results, where lattice discreteness
would predict a large, already-excluded effect.

## Why it matters

This is a distinctive, observational prediction, and a selling point. Many
quantum-gravity models predict LIV that is increasingly ruled out. Causal-set
discreteness predicts the observed ABSENCE of first-order LIV, because randomness
preserves Lorentz invariance where regularity breaks it. The discreteness scale is
bounded only from below by the null results, and the residual effect of discreteness
is not coherent LIV but the stochastic swerve (P26).
